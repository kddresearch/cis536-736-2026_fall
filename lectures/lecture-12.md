## Slide 2: The Limits of Traditional UV Mapping
### The 2D Parameterization Problem
* **UV Unwrapping:** The process of flattening 3D geometry into a 2D coordinate space (U and V).
* **Resolution Boundaries:** Bitmap textures (PNG, TGA) have fixed pixel dimensions (e.g., 2048x2048).
* **Texel Density:** Scaling up a mesh stretches the texture, revealing pixelation.
* **The Core Limitation:** Traditional UVs rely on static, finite external assets that consume VRAM and cannot adapt dynamically to scaling.

**Speaker Notes:**
Standard materials rely on UV unwrapping. You take a 3D mesh and peel it like an orange to lay it flat on a 2D grid. The problem is that images have finite pixels. If you take a 2k brick texture and stretch it across a massive fortress wall, your texel density drops. The image blurs, the illusion breaks, and you are hard-capped by the VRAM required to load higher-resolution textures. 

---

## Slide 3: Seams and Stretching
### Geometric Constraints in UV Space
* **Seams:** The visible boundary where the 2D unwrap is cut. Texture patterns often fail to align across these cuts.
* **Stretching:** Complex curvature (like terrain or organic shapes) forces distortion in the 2D projection.
* **Tiling Artifacts:** Repeating a texture to increase resolution creates obvious, repeating visual grids.

**Speaker Notes:**
Even if you tile a texture to preserve resolution, you create a new problem: the tiling artifact. The human eye is incredibly good at spotting repeating grids. Furthermore, any complex object requires cuts—seams—to flatten it. If a brick pattern crosses a seam, the bricks won't align. For organic shapes like caves or procedural terrain, manual UV unwrapping is often mathematically impossible without severe stretching.

## Slide 4: Procedural Noise Functions
### Mathematical Generation over Pixel Sampling
* **Procedural Textures:** Generated by mathematical algorithms at runtime within the GPU fragment context.
* **Infinite Resolution:** Because they are math, not pixels, zooming in never reveals pixelation.
* **Non-Repeating:** Uses coordinate math (often World Space or Object Space) to generate organic, non-tiling patterns.
* **Memory Efficient:** Costs compute cycles rather than VRAM.

**Speaker Notes:**
The solution to the UV limit is procedural generation. Instead of sampling an image pixel, the shader calculates a mathematical function for every fragment on the screen. Because it's pure math, it has infinite resolution. You can zoom in forever and it will never pixelate. It also costs virtually zero VRAM, trading memory footprint for GPU compute cycles, which modern hardware handles with ease.

## Slide 5: Types of Procedural Noise in Shader Graph
### Perlin, Value, and Voronoi
* **Simple Noise (Value/Perlin variants):** Interpolated gradients creating smooth, cloudy, or dirt-like organic gradients.
* **Voronoi Noise:** Cellular noise based on distance to nearest seed points.
* **Visual Characteristics:** 
  * *Simple:* Fog, dirt, rust, organic blending.
  * *Voronoi:* Scales, cracked earth, water caustics, cellular tissue.

**Speaker Notes:**
Unity's Shader Graph comes with built-in procedural noise nodes. "Simple Noise" gives you a cloudy, smooth gradient excellent for dirt, fog, or blending maps. "Voronoi" generates cellular structures by calculating the distance to random points in space. It is your go-to algorithm for cracked earth, reptilian scales, or the light caustics at the bottom of a swimming pool.

## Slide 6: Triplanar Mapping
### Escaping UV Space
* **The Concept:** Projecting a texture onto a mesh from three planar directions (X, Y, and Z) simultaneously.
* **World Space Independence:** Triplanar evaluates the position of the pixel in the 3D world, ignoring the mesh's UV coordinates entirely.
* **Use Cases:** 
  * Procedurally generated terrain.
  * Complex caves or cliffs.
  * Objects where UV unwrapping is impossible or distorted.

**Speaker Notes:**
If we have seamless noise, how do we apply it to a mesh that has terrible or missing UVs? We use Triplanar Mapping. Instead of reading the 2D UV map, Triplanar mapping acts like three slide projectors pointing at the object from the X, Y, and Z axes. Because it maps using the object's actual 3D coordinates in the world, the texture sticks to the geometry perfectly, regardless of how badly the model was unwrapped.

## Slide 7: Triplanar Blending Mechanics
### Normal-Driven Projections
* **World Normals:** Triplanar mapping uses the surface normal (the direction the polygon faces) to determine which projection to show.
* **Blending:** 
  * Surfaces facing UP (Y) get the top projection (e.g., snow or grass).
  * Surfaces facing sideways (X, Z) get the side projection (e.g., rock).
* **The Triplanar Node:** Unity encapsulates this into a single node, blending the seams seamlessly.

**Speaker Notes:**
The magic of Triplanar isn't just three projectors; it's how they blend. The shader looks at the World Normal of the pixel. If the pixel points straight up, it gives it 100% of the Y-projection. If it points to the side, it gives it the X or Z. In the areas where they meet, it blends them together smoothly. This is exactly how games instantly put snow on the top of dynamic rocks, regardless of how you rotate them in the level.

## Slide 8: Masking & Blending Materials
### Noise as a Lerp Threshold
* **Linear Interpolation (Lerp):** Blends between Input A and Input B based on a parameter T (0 to 1).
* **Noise Masking:** Using grayscale procedural noise (values 0 to 1) as the T input.
* **Result:** Dynamically blends two distinct materials (e.g., clean metal vs. rust) based on an organic, non-repeating pattern.
* **Parameterization:** Exposing scale and offset to the Blackboard for art direction.

**Speaker Notes:**
Noise isn't just for visuals; it's for logic. On Monday, we covered the Lerp node—blending between A and B based on a threshold T. If we plug our procedural noise into the T port, the black areas (0) will show Material A, and the white areas (1) will show Material B. This is how we procedurally generate rust on metal or moss on stone. By exposing the noise scale to the Blackboard, we let our artists control the rust density directly from the Unity Inspector.

## Slide 9: Vertex Displacement
### Modifying Geometry at Runtime
* **Vertex Context vs. Fragment Context:** Moving upstream in the rendering pipeline.
* **The Math:** `Vertex Position + (World Normal * Noise Value) = Displacement`
* **Execution:** We multiply our procedural noise by the vertex's normal vector, pushing the vertex outward along its facing direction.
* **Result:** Physical, runtime deformation of the mesh silhouette (waves, breathing organic matter, pulsating magic).

**Speaker Notes:**
Up until now, we have only worked in the Fragment context—changing the color of pixels. But Shader Graph also gives us access to the Vertex context. We can actually move the 3D geometry of the mesh before it gets rendered. By taking our procedural noise, multiplying it by the vertex normal, and adding it to the vertex position, we push the geometry outward. This is how you create ocean waves, pulsating alien eggs, or vibrating force fields.
